How to Tell if a Dating Profile Is Fake

The old advice — reverse image search, look for weird fingers — stopped working around 2023. Here's what actually catches fakes now.

A friend of mine matched with a woman on Hinge last spring. Three photos. Gym selfie, coffee shop, hiking trip in what looked like Colorado. Bio said she was a pediatric nurse. He was into it. They messaged for eleven days before she brought up an opportunity in short-term crypto lending that had been "life-changing" for her.

He didn't send money. But he'd already sent enough of himself — voice notes, work stories, a photo of his dog. She wasn't real. None of the photos were. The face in all three had been generated the same afternoon the profile was created.

This is not a rare story. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center logged $672,009,052 in confidence and romance scam losses across 17,910 complaints in 2024. The FTC, using broader self-reported data, put romance fraud at $1.14 billion across 64,003 reports. Median individual loss on the FTC data: $2,000. That's a car repair, a month of rent, a chunk of someone's savings — gone.

The tactics have shifted fast. Below is what still works to spot a fake, what doesn't work anymore, and the specific behaviors scammers can't fake even when their photos are perfect.

Reverse image search stopped being the silver bullet

For years, the standard advice was: drop the profile photo into Google Images or TinEye and see where else it appears. If the same face showed up on a modeling agency site in Brazil, you had your answer.

That advice is now half-useful at best. Scammers moved off stolen photos because they got caught too often. The switch was to AI-generated faces from tools like Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, and dozens of face-specific generators that produce photorealistic portraits in under thirty seconds.

An AI-generated face has no history. It doesn't exist on any modeling site, any Instagram account, any LinkedIn. Reverse image search returns nothing — and people read that "nothing" as reassurance. It's the opposite. A profile whose photos have zero web footprint is now a yellow flag, not a green light.

Reverse search still catches lazy scammers who lifted photos from a real person's Instagram. Run it anyway. Just don't stop there.

The photo set tells you more than any single photo

Most people think you spot a fake by staring hard at one image looking for warped ears or six fingers. The generators fixed most of that by late 2023. What they still struggle with is consistency across a set.

Look at the profile as a whole. Ask specific questions.

Does the same person actually appear in all the photos? Not "looks similar" — the same person. Check the ear shape, the distance between the eyes, the exact hairline. AI-generated sets often use the same seed with variations, and the face drifts subtly between images. A real person's face doesn't drift.

Are there any candid photos with other people? Fake profiles almost never have group shots. Generating a convincing image of your target and three specific friends at a wedding is hard. Solo posed shots are cheap.

Is there any photo showing readable text? Menus, signs, book covers, tattoos, tournament bibs. Text is where AI generators still fall apart. Garbled letters in the background of a "coffee shop" photo is a giveaway. So is a t-shirt logo that's almost a brand you recognize but not quite.

Reddit's r/OnlineDating regulars have been saying the same thing for a while: fake profiles feel curated the way a stock photo shoot feels curated. Real people have a bad photo in the mix. Fakes rarely do.

The tell is in the conversation, not the pictures

On nearly every "is this profile fake" thread, the top comments land on the same point: if a scammer got past the app's initial screening, their photos already looked normal. The conversation is the giveaway.

Here's what to actually watch for once you start messaging.

The pacing is off. Real people match, exchange a few lines over a couple of days, forget about it, come back. Scammers push for constant contact fast. They want to move to WhatsApp, Telegram, or Google Chat within 48 hours. The reason is boring: dating apps flag scam patterns and can nuke the account. Off-platform, no one's watching.

The details don't stick. Ask what their sister's name is on Tuesday. Ask again the following Monday phrased differently. Scammers running multiple profiles lose track. Real people don't forget their own siblings.

They dodge live video. Not "I'm shy on camera" — genuine dodging. Excuses about broken cameras, bad wifi, work travel, time zones that don't quite make sense. Deepfake video is now good enough to fool a casual video call in poor lighting, but most scammers running volume don't bother with it. If someone won't do a two-minute FaceTime after two weeks of conversation, that's your answer.

The story is too aligned with you. You mention you love hiking. Suddenly they hiked Patagonia last year. You mention your dad passed. Their dad also passed. Real people have their own interests that don't perfectly mirror yours. Scammers are trained to build rapport by matching.

The money ask always comes, and it always comes wrapped

Nobody running a romance scam opens with "send me $500." The whole point of the weeks of buildup is to make the ask feel like a favor between people who care about each other.

The wrapping varies. A stuck shipment that needs a customs fee. A medical emergency for a parent. A crypto investment opportunity that's "already paid for their car." A plane ticket to finally come visit. A locked account they just need to unfreeze.

The FTC's data on this is clear: cryptocurrency and wire transfers are the top payment methods in reported romance fraud, precisely because they're irreversible. Nobody legitimate who cares about you will ask you to buy gift cards, wire money to a third party, or send USDT to a wallet address. Ever.

And the ask doesn't have to be for money to be a scam. Sometimes it's for photos, for your address, for a copy of your ID "to verify you're real too." All of that has resale value.

What actually works: layer the checks

No single test catches every fake. Layer three or four and almost none get through.

Run the photos through reverse image search. If they show up on someone else's real account, done. If they show up nowhere at all, treat that as a data point, not an all-clear.

Run at least one photo through an AI detector. This is where a tool like Faux Spy exists — right-click the photo in your browser and get a probability score in seconds. Diffusion-generated faces leave statistical fingerprints that detectors catch even when your eyes can't.

Ask for a live video call within the first two weeks. Not a scheduled one — a spontaneous one. "Hey, quick hi?" A scammer running fifty profiles can't take that call. A real person can, even if they look terrible.

Watch the pacing and the platform pressure. If they're pushing off-app fast and love-bombing hard, slow it down and see what happens. Real interest survives a slower pace. Scam interest doesn't — the scammer moves to the next target because time is their cost.

Trust the small weird feeling. The one where the messages sound slightly translated. The one where a detail doesn't match. The one where the photos are all somehow lit the same way. That feeling is pattern recognition doing its job. Don't argue yourself out of it.

The uncomfortable part

Fake profiles work because loneliness is real and attention feels good. Nobody gets scammed because they're stupid. People get scammed because someone spent three weeks making them feel seen.

The best defense isn't paranoia. It's a habit of verification before emotional investment gets expensive. Check the photos early, when it costs you nothing to walk away. Ask for the video call before you've told them about your childhood. Assume the first money ask, however it's wrapped, is the whole point of the conversation.

If it turns out they're real, none of these checks cost you anything. If they're not, they save you the $2,000 the median victim loses — and the part that's harder to get back.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the signs of a dating scammer?

They quickly profess feelings, avoid video calls, ask for money or personal info, and have vague or inconsistent stories. They often use stolen photos and create urgency to meet or help with emergencies.

How to verify a dating profile?

Request a video call early on. Do a reverse image search on their photos to check if they're stolen. Ask specific questions about their life and see if answers are consistent.

What are some telltale signs of a fake dating profile?

Generic bio, professional-looking photos that seem like stock images, no activity history, sparse details, and messaging patterns that feel robotic or copy-pasted.

What are red flags for fake profiles?

Moving conversations off the app quickly, refusing video verification, love-bombing, requesting money or payment info, and profiles with only one or two photos that look overly polished.

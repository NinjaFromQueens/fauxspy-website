How to Tell If a Dating Profile Is Fake

Reverse image search used to be the whole game. Against AI-generated faces, it returns zero results — because the person doesn't exist.

Quick Answer

A fake dating profile usually shows a mix of these signs: too few photos (or too many that look studio-perfect), a recently created account, immediate push to move off the app, refusal to video chat, and photos that fail an AI-image check. In 2024, Americans reported losing $1.14 billion to romance scams, and the FBI confirms AI-generated photos are now standard tooling for scammers.

Last updated November 2, 2026

Here's a scene from a Hinge screenshot someone sent me last month: a woman, 29, two photos, both chest-up, both soft studio lighting. Bio is one line. She matches, says hi within four minutes, asks for his Instagram inside the first six messages. He checks the Instagram. It's three weeks old, 180 followers, every photo is her.

Classic. And the face? Generated by Stable Diffusion. He only figured that out after sending her $400 for a "stuck in Lagos" story.

The old advice — "reverse image search their photos!" — doesn't catch this anymore. The face doesn't exist anywhere else because it never existed at all. You need a different set of checks now.

How do you know if a dating profile is fake?

You look at behavior, metadata, and image artifacts together — no single sign is proof, but three or four stacked up almost always is. The FTC's romance scam data shows the median victim loss is $2,000, and most victims report that the warning signs were visible early. They just didn't know what they were looking at.

Here's what to actually check, in rough order of how fast you can do it:

Photo count and composition. Real people have messy camera rolls. They have a photo from a cousin's wedding, a bad selfie in a car, a group shot where they're half-cropped. Fake profiles tend to have 2–4 photos that are all shot like a magazine spread — same lighting quality, same face angle range, no friends, no context.

Account age. On Instagram, tap the profile → three dots → "About this account." It will tell you the join date. A match whose Instagram was created last month and has 90 followers who are all men is not a coincidence.

Response speed and script. Scammers answer fast and warm. They skip the awkward "so what do you do?" phase and jump to compliments and emotional questions by day two. They often work from a script — if their messages feel oddly polished, oddly affectionate, and oddly fast, that's three oddlys in a row.

Platform migration. They want to move to WhatsApp, Telegram, or Google Chat within 48 hours. Tinder, Bumble, and Hinge all have moderation and reporting systems. Scammers want you somewhere they can't be banned.

The one test most people skip: ask for a live video call

Not a pre-recorded video. Not a voice note. A live FaceTime, WhatsApp video, or Zoom call where you can watch them move in real time. Scammers running AI-generated profiles can't do this reliably, and the ones using deepfake video filters still have tells — the face stutters when they turn sideways, the hair clipping is weird, their hand passes "through" their chin.

Watch what happens when you ask. A real person says "sure, give me ten minutes" or "I'm shy, can we chat more first?" A scammer invents an increasingly complex reason: broken camera, remote work site with bad signal, oil rig in the North Sea, military deployment, hospital.

The excuses are the giveaway. Real shyness fades after a week. Scammer excuses escalate.

iProov documented a 704% rise in face-swap deepfake attacks on identity systems in the second half of 2023. That technology is now in the hands of romance scammers too. But it's still fragile in live, interactive video — especially if you ask them to turn their head slowly or wave a hand in front of their face.

What are the signs that a photo is AI-generated?

The obvious glitches — six fingers, melted earrings, teeth that look like a piano — are mostly gone from current-generation models like Midjourney v6 and Flux. The subtle ones remain if you know where to look.

Backgrounds. AI models care about faces, not backgrounds. Look at the stuff behind the person. Book spines that have no readable text. A doorframe that doesn't quite line up. A plant with leaves that merge into each other. A café in the background where the chairs are vaguely chair-shaped but wrong.

Jewelry and accessories. Asymmetric earrings. Watches with no numbers. Necklace chains that disappear behind the neck and reappear in the wrong place. Glasses frames that fade on one side.

Hair at the edges. Where hair meets background, AI often produces a soft haze or a too-clean cutout. Real photos have individual flyaway strands.

Skin that's too even. Most AI faces have poreless, uniform skin, even in "candid" shots. Real human skin has texture, pores, slightly uneven tone, and small blemishes that generators smooth away.

Most people assume AI faces look weird. They don't anymore. They look better than real people — and that is the tell. If someone's photos look like they walked out of a Vogue shoot but their bio says they're a 32-year-old accountant in Omaha, trust the mismatch.

How many dating profiles are actually fake?

Nobody has a clean number, because the dating apps don't want to tell you. What we do have: the FTC's 2023 romance scam report says about 40% of people who lost money to a romance scam in 2022 said the contact started on social media, and about 19% said it started on a dating app or website. The FBI's IC3 unit logged 17,910 romance scam complaints in 2024 totaling $672 million in losses.

Match Group, which owns Tinder, Hinge, OkCupid, and Match.com, says in its own transparency reports that it removes millions of fake accounts per quarter — Tinder alone removed over 44 million spam and scam accounts in a recent reporting period. That's accounts that got through signup and had to be cleaned up after.

The real base rate on any given app is probably 5–15% of profiles at any moment, with huge variance by location and demographic. If you're a man over 40 matching with women 25–35 who message first, your personal fake-profile rate is a lot higher than the platform average.

The 60-second check you can run on any profile

Before you reply, before you get invested, run this:

Save one of their photos. Reverse image search it on Google Images and TinEye. If the photo shows up on a stock site, a different person's Instagram, or an Eastern European modeling site — done, it's stolen. If it returns nothing at all and the face is unusually attractive and perfectly lit, that's a flag for AI generation, not a clearance.

Then run the same photo through an AI image detector. That's what Faux Spy does on right-click, in your browser, without uploading the image anywhere. You get a verdict in a few seconds on whether the photo was likely generated by Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, Flux, or similar models.

Then check their social presence. If they have Instagram linked, look at the account age, the follower-to-following ratio, whether anyone they know comments on their posts, and whether the photos there match the dating app photos in a believable way (different outfits, different settings, same person).

Sixty seconds. Three tools. Catches most of them.

Is it a crime to make a fake dating profile?

Making a fake profile by itself usually isn't criminal in the US — there's no federal law against lying about your identity online. But what fake profiles are typically used for is very much illegal: wire fraud, identity theft, extortion, and in some states impersonation of a real person is its own charge.

If someone uses a fake profile to con money out of you, that's wire fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1343, which carries up to 20 years in federal prison. If they're using photos of a real person without permission, several states (including California, Texas, and New York) have online impersonation statutes. Report to the FBI at ic3.gov and to the app itself. Both matter.

And don't feel stupid. These operations are professional. People running them do this full-time, often from scam compounds in Southeast Asia described in reporting by Reuters and the UN. You weren't matched against a bored person in a basement — you were matched against a team with scripts, playbooks, and KPIs.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you know if you are chatting with a scammer online?

Watch for requests for money, refusal to video chat, inconsistent stories, and generic messages sent to multiple people. Scammers often ask personal questions to build trust quickly, then pivot to financial requests or emotional manipulation.

Is it a crime to make a fake dating profile?

Yes, depending on intent and jurisdiction. Creating fake profiles for fraud, catfishing, or identity theft is illegal; however, using a pseudonym alone typically isn't criminal unless it's used to deceive for financial gain or cause harm.

How to verify a Facebook dating profile?

Check if their Facebook account has a real photo history, friends list, and posts spanning months or years. Request a video call, ask them to take a live photo with a specific pose, or search their photo on reverse image search to confirm authenticity.

How many dating profiles are fake?

Studies estimate 10-40% of dating profiles are fake, depending on the platform and methodology. Major apps actively remove bot and scam accounts, but fake profiles remain common due to catfishing, bots, and fraud schemes.

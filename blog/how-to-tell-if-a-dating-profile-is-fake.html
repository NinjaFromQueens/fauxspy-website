How to Tell If a Dating Profile Is Fake

Reverse image search stopped working two years ago. Here's what actually catches fake profiles now.

A woman in Ohio sent a man she met on Hinge $84,000 over eleven months. She never met him in person. He had six photos, a job at an oil rig off the coast of Scotland, a daughter in boarding school, and a habit of video-calling her for exactly forty seconds before "the connection dropped." Every photo was AI-generated. The video calls were a deepfake loop. She reported it in early 2025.

Her case isn't rare. The FTC logged $1.14 billion in romance scam losses across 64,003 reports in 2024, with a median loss of $2,000 per victim. The FBI's IC3 numbers show biometric fraud — the deepfake stuff — surged 704% year over year.

Most advice on spotting fake profiles is five years out of date. Reverse image search barely works anymore because scammers generate faces that have never existed. "Look for extra fingers" stopped being useful around the time Midjourney v5 shipped. The real tells are elsewhere.

The photos are too consistent, not too weird

People assume fake profiles look off. Bad lighting, weird backgrounds, obvious airbrushing. That's the old model. The new one is the opposite problem: every photo looks like it was taken by the same photographer on the same afternoon.

Real people have messy camera rolls. A selfie in a car. A photo their sister took at a wedding three years ago. A blurry group shot from a bar. A picture where they look tired. When you scroll a real profile on Tinder or Bumble, the photos span years, moods, and image quality.

Fake profiles don't have this. The subject is always the same age, same weight, same haircut, same skin tone, same lighting temperature. Backgrounds are generic or contradictory — someone claiming to live in Denver with three photos in front of what looks like a Mediterranean coastline.

Check the earrings. Check the necklace. Check the collar of the shirt. AI generators are still bad at maintaining accessory consistency across images of "the same" person. If she's wearing gold hoops in every photo but one shows silver studs with the same outfit, that's the tell.

The bio reads like a job application, not a person

Scammers write bios that hit demographic buttons. Widowed. Military or offshore worker. One kid. Loves God, family, and travel. Looking for "someone honest and God-fearing." The phrasing repeats across thousands of profiles because it's copied from templates traded on Telegram groups.

Real bios on Hinge and Bumble have specifics. A weird band. A hyper-local restaurant. A hobby that requires explanation. An inside joke that doesn't quite land. Fake bios stay abstract on purpose — specifics are things scammers can be caught on later.

Watch for the profession combo: oil rig engineer, orthopedic surgeon deployed with the UN, cryptocurrency trader, general in the Army. These jobs get used because they explain why he can't meet you, why he's abroad, why his schedule is erratic, and why he might suddenly need money.

The AARP tracked romance scam cases in 2023 and found "military deployed overseas" was the single most common cover story, followed by "offshore worker" and "doctor with an international NGO." If his job sounds like an excuse in advance, it probably is.

They move off the app in under 48 hours

This is the most reliable behavioral signal, and it's been true for a decade. A real match will happily chat on Tinder or Hinge for a week before suggesting a phone number. A scammer wants you on WhatsApp, Telegram, or Google Chat almost immediately.

Why? Dating apps scan messages for scam patterns. Match Group (which owns Tinder, Hinge, OKCupid, and Plenty of Fish) removed over 44 million fake accounts in 2023 alone. Once you're on WhatsApp, no one is monitoring. The scammer can send links, request money, or run a deepfake video call without any platform interference.

The pitch is always soft. "This app is buggy for me." "I barely check here." "Text me at this number, it's easier." All of these translate to: get somewhere I can't be banned from.

Stay on the app until you've had a real video call. Not a forty-second one where the connection drops. A real one, with movement and conversation.

The video call is the moment of truth

Deepfake video calls exist. They're getting better. But in 2026, they still have limits that a normal conversation exposes fast.

Ask them to turn their head fully sideways. Real-time deepfakes are trained mostly on front-facing footage — profile angles break the model. You'll see the face warp, the ear disappear, or the skin texture smear.

Ask them to wave a hand in front of their face. The deepfake overlay struggles with occlusion. Fingers will blur into the cheek, or the hand will appear to pass behind the face when it should be in front.

Ask them to hold up a specific number of fingers. Or to write a word on a piece of paper and show the camera. These aren't things a scammer running a pre-recorded loop or an AI face filter can improvise.

And if they refuse video entirely? That's your answer. A real person who's been talking to you for two weeks will get on a video call. Someone who has excuses every time — bad internet, work schedule, camera broken, shy — is protecting something.

The money ask is not always dramatic

Everyone knows the "I need $5,000 for a plane ticket" scam. The successful scams in 2025 look different. They start small. They frame the request as a favor, a temporary loan, or an investment opportunity you'd be crazy to miss.

The FTC reports that cryptocurrency is now the payment method in the highest-loss romance scam category, with a median individual loss of $10,000 — five times the overall romance scam median. The pitch is a trading platform he uses, or an app her cousin runs, or a mining opportunity that closes Friday.

Gift cards are the second most common ask, especially for lower-income targets. Steam cards, Apple gift cards, Google Play. These are untraceable and instantly resellable.

The rule is simple. No one you have never met in person should ever receive money from you. Not for a plane ticket, not for a medical bill, not for a "sure thing" investment. Not even $50 to prove you trust them. Especially not that.

The photo test that still works

Reverse image search on Google or TinEye used to catch stolen photos. It still catches lazy scammers who grab a model's Instagram. But AI-generated faces return zero matches because the face doesn't exist anywhere else — and scammers know this.

So instead of asking "has this photo been used elsewhere," ask "was this photo generated by a machine." Different question, different tool.

AI detection browser extensions can analyze the photo directly in the browser without you saving or uploading anything. Right-click the profile photo, run the check, get a probability score. It takes about three seconds and catches the majority of AI-generated profile pictures produced by Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, FLUX, and the various StyleGAN derivatives that scammer toolkits use.

Combine that with the behavioral signals above and you'll catch nearly every fake profile before it costs you anything.

What to do if you think you're being scammed

Stop sending messages. Not "confront them and see how they react" — scammers are trained for the confrontation script and will manipulate you back in. Just stop.

Screenshot everything before you block. Profile, photos, message history, any phone numbers or wallet addresses. Report to the platform (Tinder, Hinge, Bumble, and Match all have report flows that actually work). Report to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI at IC3.gov if any money changed hands.

If you sent cryptocurrency, report to the exchange you used within 24 hours. Some funds have been recovered when reports come in fast enough for the receiving wallet to still hold the balance.

And talk to someone. Romance scam victims report crushing shame, and that shame is what keeps them from getting help or warning others. The scammer is the criminal. You are one of hundreds of thousands of people who got targeted this year by an industry that has gotten very good at what it does.

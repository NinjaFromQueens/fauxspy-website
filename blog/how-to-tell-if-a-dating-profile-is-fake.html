How to Tell If a Dating Profile Is Fake: 9 Real Signs

The photos aren't the hard part anymore. The tell is in the pattern of what they will and won't do in the first 48 hours.

Quick Answer

A fake dating profile usually fails three tests: the photos don't exist anywhere else on the web (or exist in too many places under different names), the person refuses a live video call on short notice, and the conversation moves to WhatsApp or Telegram within days. Add a sudden money ask, a vague bio, and a job that conveniently keeps them abroad, and you're almost certainly looking at a scammer or a bot. The FTC logged $1.14 billion in romance scam losses in 2024.

Last updated November 16, 2026

Last month a friend showed me a Hinge profile she'd been chatting with for nine days. Oil rig engineer. Widowed. Daughter at boarding school in Switzerland. Six photos, all slightly different lighting, all the same guy with the same tight smile. She wanted to know if he was real.

He wasn't.

The photos passed a basic reverse image search. What gave him away was something simpler — he'd never been on a video call in his life, apparently, and his texts had the rhythm of someone translating from another language and then running it through a tone filter. That's the pattern. The tech got better. The scripts didn't.

How can you tell if a dating profile is real or AI?

Check whether the face exists anywhere else on the internet and whether it has the small imperfections real photos have. AI-generated faces from Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, and similar tools produce no reverse image search hits because the face is new. Real stolen photos, on the other hand, usually produce too many hits — the same face attached to a model's Instagram, a stock photo site, or a different name on LinkedIn.

Here's what to actually look at in the image:

Earrings and glasses. Diffusion models still mangle paired objects. One earring will be a stud, the other a hoop. Glasses frames will shift thickness between the left and right side. Look closely.

Background text. Signs, book spines, posters in the background — AI almost never gets readable text right. You'll see letter-shaped smears that look like a language but aren't one.

Teeth and ears. Teeth often blur into a single white shape. Ears sometimes have extra folds or missing cartilage. Hair strands near the ear can melt into the jawline.

Skin texture. Real skin has pores, stray hairs, slight asymmetry. AI skin tends to look airbrushed even in "candid" shots. If the person has flawless skin in a bathroom selfie under overhead lighting, that's a flag.

Most people think the eyes are the giveaway. They're wrong. Modern generators handle eyes well. The hands, ears, and background are where it still falls apart.

What are the signs of a dating scammer?

Behavioral signs are more reliable than visual ones, because scripts age slower than image models. The FTC's 2024 data pegged the median individual loss for romance fraud at $2,000, with over 64,000 reports. Those victims mostly didn't get caught by a bad photo. They got caught by a believable conversation.

Watch for these:

They escalate emotionally in under a week. "I've never felt this connection before." "You're different from anyone I've ever met." They haven't met you. They've exchanged 200 texts with you. Real people don't say that. Scammers do, because the script tells them to.

They push off-platform fast. Tinder, Hinge, and Bumble have moderation. WhatsApp and Telegram don't. If they're asking for your number or a Telegram handle within the first day or two, that's not eagerness. That's them trying to get you somewhere they can't be banned.

Their job keeps them unreachable. Oil rig. Military deployment. Doctor with Doctors Without Borders. UN contractor. Freight captain. Any job that explains why they can't video chat and why they might suddenly need money for emergency equipment.

They mirror you too well. You mention you love hiking. Suddenly their old photos involve hiking. You mention a dog. They have a dog now too, apparently. Real compatibility doesn't form that cleanly.

Their photos don't match their life. A 48-year-old engineer who only has gym selfies and professional headshots. No photos with friends. No photos at a wedding, at a party, in a car, holding a coffee. Real people have mess in their camera rolls. Scammers have curation.

The video call test is the single best filter

Ask for a short video call on FaceTime, WhatsApp, or the dating app's built-in video. Not scheduled for next week. Now, or tonight. A real person says sure, or says they're busy but suggests tomorrow. A scammer invents a reason — bad internet, phone broken, at work, camera won't work on their laptop.

If they do agree to video, watch the face carefully. Deepfake face-swap tools exist and are getting used. iProov's 2024 threat intelligence report documented a 704% rise in face-swap attacks on identity verification in the second half of 2023. The tells on a live face-swap: the face stutters when they turn their head quickly, the edge of the face blurs against the background, lighting on the face doesn't match the lighting of the room behind them.

Ask them to turn their head fully to one side, or to put a hand in front of their face and pull it away. Face-swap models still struggle with occlusion. Watch what happens at the edges.

Most scammers won't get this far. The ones that do are the expensive ones — the ones running six-figure pig-butchering operations, not the $200-wire scammer.

Can you find out who is behind a fake profile?

Sometimes, but usually not the person — only the pattern. Reverse image search the photos on Google Images, TinEye, and Yandex (Yandex is often best for faces). If you get hits, you'll see where the real photos were stolen from. Common sources: Russian or Ukrainian Instagram accounts, Chinese modeling agencies, LinkedIn profiles of military personnel, dead social media accounts.

If the photos return zero hits across all three engines, that's a signal the face is AI-generated, not that the person is real. A real person with six photos on a dating app has almost always posted at least one of them somewhere else.

You can also search unique phrases from their bio in quotes on Google. Scammers reuse bios. A line like "looking for my queen to spoil for life" will often return a dozen other profiles word-for-word.

Identifying the actual human running the account is nearly impossible without law enforcement resources. These operations run out of scam compounds in Southeast Asia, call centers in Nigeria, and organized groups across Eastern Europe. The FBI's 2024 IC3 report tracked $672 million in confidence and romance scam losses, and the actual perpetrators behind most of that money will never be named.

Your job isn't to identify them. Your job is to stop talking to them.

The small behavioral tests that work

Try these. They're cheap and they work.

Ask for a selfie doing something specific. "Can you send me a selfie holding up three fingers?" A real person laughs and sends it in two minutes. A scammer either refuses, stalls, or sends an older photo that doesn't match. They can't generate that specific pose on demand from a stolen photo library.

Reference something they said and get it slightly wrong. If they told you they grew up in Portland, ask later how they liked growing up in Seattle. A real person corrects you immediately. A scammer running three conversations at once might not catch it, or might roll with the wrong city because they've lost track.

Name a local place. If they claim to live in your city, ask what their favorite coffee shop near a specific intersection is. Real locals have an opinion. Scammers using Google Maps have a delay and a weirdly generic answer.

Switch languages briefly. If they claim to be from a country with a non-English native language, send one message in that language. Not a hard sentence — a greeting, a joke. Most romance scammers working English-language profiles do not actually speak the language their fake persona claims to be native in.

What to do if you've already been talking to one

Stop sending money. Stop sending photos they could use against you. Don't tell them you think they're a scammer — they'll just pivot to crisis mode and try to guilt one more payment out of you. Just go quiet.

Report the profile on the platform. Tinder, Hinge, Bumble, and Match all have scam-report flows, and they do actually ban accounts. Then report to the FBI IC3 and the FTC. Even if you didn't lose money, your report feeds the pattern data that catches the next ring.

If you sent money by wire or gift card, call the sending institution immediately. Sometimes it can be clawed back if you move within hours. Crypto almost never can.

And forgive yourself. These scripts are refined on millions of victims. The scam worked on you because it's engineered to. That's not the same as being gullible.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the signs of a dating scammer?

Scammers typically avoid video calls, ask for money quickly, have generic or stolen photos, and rush into declarations of love. They'll also make excuses for why they can't meet in person or consistently ask for personal information.

How to tell if a profile is real or AI?

Reverse image search the photos—AI-generated faces often appear flawless with uncanny details like odd ear shapes or asymmetrical features. Real profiles usually have candid, imperfect photos with consistent lighting and backgrounds across multiple images.

Can you find out who is behind a fake profile?

You can reverse search their photos, check their metadata, or report them to the platform for investigation. Most dating apps won't disclose user identity, but law enforcement can subpoena that information if it's part of a scam investigation.

How to test a romance scammer?

Suggest a video call or in-person meeting—scammers will always decline or delay. Ask specific questions about their photos or life details and watch for inconsistencies in their answers.

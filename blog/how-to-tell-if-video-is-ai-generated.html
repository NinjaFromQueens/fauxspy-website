How to Tell If a Video Is AI-Generated

Single frames of AI video can look completely real now, and many clips come with matching sound. The giveaways are in the motion, the audio and where the video came from. Here's how to check, step by step.

Quick Answer

To tell if a video is AI-generated, slow it down and watch the motion, not single frames: backgrounds that shimmer, hands and text that change between frames, objects that appear or vanish, and sound that doesn't fit the room. Then look for a watermark or an "AI-generated" label, check a paused frame with an AI image detector, and find out who posted it first. No single check is proof, so combine them.

Is this video AI? A 7-step check

Work through these in order. Each one takes under a minute, and together they catch far more than any single test.

  1. Slow it down. AI mistakes hide in motion, so play the clip at 0.25x speed. On YouTube in a desktop browser, pause and press the period and comma keys to step forward and back one frame at a time.
  2. Watch the background and edges. In real footage, walls and floors stay still. In AI video they often shimmer, drift or warp, especially where a person meets the background.
  3. Watch hands, text and small objects. Fingers that merge or change count, signs whose letters rearrange, a cup that moves or disappears between shots.
  4. Listen. Many generators now make the sound too. Check whether the lips really match the words, whether the voice ever breathes, and whether the echo fits the room.
  5. Look for a watermark or label. A generator's logo in a corner, or a platform tag like TikTok's "AI-generated" label. A missing one proves nothing, because watermarks get cropped and labels get stripped.
  6. Check a paused frame. Pause on the clearest shot of a face or scene, take a screenshot, and upload it to a free AI image checker like Faux Spy's. A high AI score is a serious warning sign; a clean frame doesn't clear the whole video.
  7. Check the source. Who posted it first? Look for the original upload, a creator who labels their work as AI, or a fact-check. A screenshot in Google Lens can turn up earlier copies.

Why AI video got harder to spot

Early AI video gave itself away within seconds: faces warped at the edges, backgrounds flickered, and the picture had a soft, smeared look. That changed in 2025. Generators started producing crisp footage with steady camera moves, and some began creating matching sound. Google's Veo 3, announced in May 2025, was the first Google model to generate video with audio, from street noise to dialogue between characters.

The tools keep changing, too. OpenAI shut down its Sora app in April 2026, but clips made with it are still shared every day, often with the watermark cropped out. So don't judge a video by which generator might have made it. Judge it by what you can see, hear and trace.

The good news: the tells haven't disappeared. They're subtler, and you have to know where to look instead of waiting for something to jump out.

Visual tells that still hold up

Background drift. In real footage, static things stay static. In AI video, backgrounds often have a gentle wobble or texture change between frames that no real camera would add. Find a moment where nothing in the foreground moves and watch a patch of wall or floor for a few seconds. A slight pulse or shimmer is a strong hint.

Lighting that doesn't commit. Real light comes from somewhere. It hits surfaces at a consistent angle and casts consistent shadows. AI lighting tends to be plausible rather than physically right: a face lit from slightly different directions during one conversation, or a room whose light shifts between shots for no reason.

Hands and fingers. Still one of the more reliable tells. Single frames can look fine, but watch a hand pick something up, gesture or type. Fingers appear and disappear, and proportions shift. The wrongness is in the motion.

Object permanence. A real coffee cup stays where it was put, with the same shadow. In AI video, small objects drift, vanish briefly or change shape, especially when they leave the frame and come back.

Text. Signs, labels, screens and T-shirt slogans are hard for generators to keep consistent. Pause on any text, step a few frames, and see if the letters change.

Hair and fabric. Watch how hair moves when someone turns, or how clothing reacts when they sit. AI physics is often close but slightly off, as if gravity were a little different. Real hair has random flyaways; AI hair often moves in neat, uniform waves.

Listen as well as watch

Now that generators can produce sound, the audio deserves its own check. Turn the volume up, or use headphones.

Lip sync. Watch the mouth on sounds like "b", "m" and "p", where the lips have to close. AI often gets the rhythm right but the shapes slightly wrong.

Breathing and pauses. Real people breathe, hesitate and trip over words. A voice that runs on in perfect, unbroken sentences is worth a second look.

Room sound. A voice recorded in a car, a kitchen or outdoors carries that space's echo and background noise. AI audio is often too clean for the scene, or has background noise that loops.

What doesn't work for detecting AI video?

The "fuzzy look" test. Early AI video had a soft, watercolor quality. Today's leading generators produce sharp footage, so looking for blur or over-smoothed skin will miss most of what's out there.

Waiting for obvious glitches. Melting faces and warped edges were common in 2022 and 2023. They're rare now. Expecting a visible glitch is like checking an AI portrait for six fingers: the models have been trained to avoid it.

Gut feeling. It feels reliable, but the leading generators are built to look real to people. Use your instinct as a reason to investigate, never as a conclusion either way.

Watermarks, labels and metadata

Visible watermarks. Some generators stamp their videos. OpenAI's Sora app added a visible, moving watermark to downloaded videos. Watermarks are easy to crop or blur, so their absence tells you nothing.

Invisible watermarks. Google marks everything made with Veo with SynthID, a watermark you can't see. It only covers Google's own tools, and you can't check it just by watching.

Platform labels. TikTok automatically labels AI videos that carry Content Credentials, and other platforms ask creators to disclose realistic AI content. A label is useful when it's there. Most AI videos don't have one, because the data it depends on is easily lost.

Metadata. In theory, a file's embedded data shows where it came from. In practice, it's stripped at almost every step: download from one app, upload to another, and it's gone. It also proves nothing the other way, since metadata can be edited. Content Credentials (C2PA) are designed to survive this, but most video in circulation doesn't carry them yet.

Is there an AI video detector?

Yes. Dedicated detectors analyze many frames of a clip, plus the motion between them, looking for statistical patterns in the pixels that AI generation leaves behind and a camera doesn't. Because they work on the pixels themselves, they can still find signals after a video has been compressed and re-uploaded.

They have limits. Very short clips, low-quality copies that have been re-uploaded many times, and footage from brand-new generators all make results less certain. A good detector gives you a confidence score rather than a yes or no, and an "inconclusive" result means it couldn't tell, not that the video is real.

Where you're most likely to meet AI video

TikTok and Instagram Reels. Short, vertical, made to be shared: exactly what AI video tools produce best. Most of it is entertainment or satire, but the format lets a fake go viral before anyone checks it.

X and news feeds. AI clips with a political angle: a public figure saying something provocative, or "footage" of an event. They can reach millions of people before a fact-check catches up.

Group chats, Reddit and Facebook groups. AI video often arrives here after going viral elsewhere, stripped of its original context and of the replies that already debunked it.

Dating apps and DMs. A short video hello is more convincing than a photo, which is exactly why scammers use them. If someone you've only met online sends a video that feels rehearsed or too perfect, run the checks above, and ask for a live video call where they do something you choose.

What to do with what you find

Weigh the evidence against what the video claims to be. A clip shared as breaking news, evidence or someone's real life deserves the most scrutiny. A clip that's clearly a joke or labeled as AI art matters much less.

One warning sign is a reason to slow down. Two or three, like drifting backgrounds, a voice that never breathes and a high AI score on a frame, are a reason not to share it, and not to send money or personal details to whoever sent it.

How can I tell if a video is AI-generated?

Can AI-generated videos have sound?

Yes. Since 2025 several generators create sound with the video, including Google's Veo 3, which can add background noise, sound effects and dialogue. Listen for lips that don't quite match the words, voices with no breaths, and room sound that doesn't fit the space.

Does video metadata reveal if a video was made by AI?

Rarely. Most videos are re-encoded when they're shared, which strips or overwrites the original metadata. Some tools and platforms add Content Credentials (C2PA) that record how a video was made, but they're easily lost. Treat metadata as a bonus signal, never as proof either way.

Can AI-generated video fool you on a live video call?

Pre-recorded or real-time deepfakes are sometimes used on video calls to impersonate someone. Ask the person to do something specific and unexpected: turn their head fully to the side, wave a hand in front of their face, or hold up an object you name. Glitches around the face, freezing, or refusing are strong warning signs.

Why doesn't reverse video search catch AI videos?

Reverse search matches a video against copies already online. An AI-generated video is new, so there's usually nothing to match. Reverse search is still useful for finding who posted a clip first, or whether a real video has been taken out of context, but it can't tell you whether footage was generated.
